What Counts as a Mining or Trades Job?

Before you start applying, it helps to know what employers mean when they post operator, underground, or trades roles. Not every listing on a generic board is a real on-site opening — and not every “mining” post is the same type of work.

On-site vs camp vs FIFO

Type What it means Best for
Local on-site Daily commute to a mine, plant, or construction site near your home. Workers living in or near active mining regions
Camp / FIFO Fly-in fly-out or bus-in rotations with employer-provided camp accommodation. Workers willing to travel for higher pay and structured rotations
Project / shutdown Short-term trades work during mill maintenance, construction, or turnaround. Red Seal trades and contractors seeking contract income

Who hires on-site?

  • Mining companies — open pit, underground, and processing operations
  • Contractors — earthworks, shaft sinking, mill maintenance, construction
  • Industrial employers — smelters, mills, power plants, and heavy industry

The “real job vs noise” problem

Generic boards are flooded with reposted listings, recruiter spam, and roles that already filled. Watch for vague posts with no company name, no location, or unrealistic pay. That is why a mining and trades job board saves hours compared to scrolling Indeed or LinkedIn — you spend less time on roles that were never meant for site workers.

Mining & Trades Hiring: Trends and Outlook (2026)

Demand across the USA and Canada

On-site mining and skilled trades remain strong across North America. Gold, copper, lithium, and industrial minerals drive hiring, while aging infrastructure and mill turnarounds keep trades contractors busy. For job seekers, that means opportunities exist — but competition for the best haul truck, underground, and Red Seal roles is real.

Northern Ontario, Quebec, BC, Saskatchewan, Nevada, Arizona, and other active districts continue to post operators, underground miners, and maintenance trades. Camp and FIFO roles often appear in remote regions where daily commuting is not practical.

Roles in consistent demand

These categories consistently post on-site openings across the USA and Canada:

  1. Heavy equipment operators — haul truck, dozer, excavator, loader
  2. Underground mining — miner, bolter, jumbo operator, raise climber
  3. Mill & plant — mill operator, process operator, metallurgist
  4. Drilling & blasting — driller, blaster, powder crew
  5. Mechanical trades — millwright, mechanic, fitter
  6. Electrical trades — electrician, instrumentation tech
  7. Welding & fabrication — welder, pipefitter, boilermaker
  8. General labor — laborer, helper, material handler
  9. Safety & geology — safety officer, geologist, environmental tech

Pay expectations

On-site mining and trades work often pays well — especially with overtime, camp premiums, and FIFO rotations. Pay varies by commodity, employer, union status, and your tickets. Entry-level labor and helper roles are a foot in the door; experienced operators and Red Seal trades command significantly more. Always confirm wages, shift structure, and benefits directly with the employer.

Types of On-Site Roles

Full-time employee

Direct hire with a mining company or contractor. You may receive benefits, training, and long-term career growth. Common for operators, underground miners, and trades at established sites.

Contract / project work

Trades contractors and specialized crews often work shutdowns, construction phases, or short-term projects. Higher day rates are common; you manage your own taxes and benefits as a contractor.

Camp and FIFO rotations

Remote sites hire workers on rotation schedules — for example 2&1, 2&2, or 14&14. Employer-provided camp, meals, and travel are typical. Confirm rotation length, travel costs, and camp rules before accepting.

Entry-level vs experienced roles

Entry-level: General labor, helper, entry operator roles with employer training. Competition is high — relevant tickets, a clean safety record, and reliability matter.

Experienced: Haul truck operators, underground miners, millwrights, electricians, and welders with proven site experience. Employers expect valid tickets, safety training, and references from prior sites.

How to Apply for Mining & Trades Jobs (and Get Interviews)

Tailor your resume for site roles

Mining HR and site supervisors scan quickly for tickets, equipment experience, and safety history. To stand out:

  • List equipment operated — CAT 793, jumbo, LHD, etc. — and years of experience
  • Include valid certifications: Common Core, MSHA, Red Seal, WHMIS, first aid
  • Quantify safety record: incident-free hours, last site, shift preference
  • Keep it one to two pages; site hiring managers want clarity, not fluff

Write a short, direct cover note

Three short paragraphs: your trade or operator background, why this site or company, and your availability (local, relocatable, or FIFO-ready). Mention tickets upfront — hiring teams need to know you can step on site.

Build proof of experience

  • Operators: Equipment types, tonnes moved, sites worked
  • Underground: Methods (cut and fill, longhole, etc.), ground support experience
  • Trades: Red Seal or journeyman status, shutdown and project history
  • All roles: Strong references from supervisors or foremen

Prepare for site interviews

Expect questions about safety, shift work, camp life, and equipment. Be ready to discuss your last site, why you left, and whether you can pass a drug and alcohol test and site orientation. Common questions include: “What equipment have you run?” and “Are you available for FIFO?”

Application workflow checklist

  1. Define 2–3 target roles (e.g. Haul Truck Operator, Underground Miner, Millwright)
  2. Update resume with tickets and equipment list
  3. Set alerts on Welcome to Trades and 1–2 company career pages
  4. Apply to quality matches weekly — tailored beats mass-applying
  5. Track applications: company, date, follow-up, status

Certifications & Safety: What You Should Know

This section is general information, not legal or safety advice. Confirm requirements with the employer and relevant regulators for your province or state.

Common training and tickets

Canada: Common Core (Ontario underground), provincial mine safety, WHMIS, first aid, and trade certifications (Red Seal millwright, electrician, welder). USA: MSHA Part 46 or 48, state-specific requirements, OSHA awareness, and trade licenses where applicable.

Site orientations and medicals

Most employers require a site-specific orientation, drug and alcohol testing, and sometimes a fit-for-work medical before your first shift. Budget time and cost for these — they are standard on active mine sites.

Cross-border work

Working in another country usually requires valid work authorization — a work permit, visa, or legal status. Do not assume a Canadian ticket alone qualifies you for US site work, or vice versa. Confirm with the employer and immigration authorities before relocating.
